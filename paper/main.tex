\begin{abstract}
This report describes a computational-aeroacoustics chain for a five-blade ducted desk fan whose trailing
edges carry owl-inspired serrations, and an audit of its acoustic output. The incompressible flow is solved
with OpenFOAM (URANS, $k$--$\omega$ SST) on a \num{1.95}-million-cell hex-dominant mesh, with the rotor in a
cyclicAMI sliding zone turning at \SI{158}{rad/s} (\SI{1509}{rpm}). The Farassat 1A form of the Ffowcs
Williams--Hawkings (FW-H) equation propagates the blade-surface pressure to four microphones at \SI{1}{m}. The
established regime gives \SI{43.3}{mN} of thrust, \SI{1.02}{mN.m} of torque and \SI{108}{m^3/h}. The spectra
show the blade-passing frequency (BPF, \SI{126}{Hz}) 20 to \SI{28}{dB} above the local floor on every
microphone, and a once-per-revolution tone that exists only in the rotor plane, with the $\cos\theta$
directivity of a compact dipole rotating with the rotor; the blade forces contain the matching rotating
in-plane component (\SI{0.95}{mN}). Re-processing the two \SI{85}{ms} FW-H records separately removed two errors
of the first analysis (a concatenation across a \SI{15}{ms} gap and a median filter that raised the floor by 9 to
\SI{24}{dB}). A compact-dipole estimate built from the integrated forces does not reproduce the FW-H levels
($+\SI{12}{dB}$ at \SI{25}{Hz}, $-\SI{18}{dB}$ at the BPF), so only tone frequencies and directivity are treated as
results. No mesh or time-step independence study was run, and the effect of the serrations is not measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The tonal noise of a low-speed fan is radiated by the unsteady force the blades exert on the air. In the
stationary frame, a rotor of $Z$ identical blades turning at $n$ revolutions per second radiates at the
blade-passing frequency $Zn$ and its harmonics; any asymmetry between blades adds tones at multiples of $n$.
Trailing-edge serrations, inspired by owl feathers, act mainly on the broadband part of the spectrum by
reducing the scattering of boundary-layer turbulence at the edge \citep{howe1991,lyu2016}. A recent study
combined owl-feather serrations with cicada-wing geometry on small propellers and reported lower overall levels
and higher propulsive efficiency \citep{wei2024}; that work was the starting point of the fan studied here.

The question of this study was practical: can an open-source chain, run on a small self-built cluster, give a
usable acoustic signature of a 3D-printable desk fan before it is built, and which parts of that signature
can be trusted? The fan has five serrated blades in a short duct, with a motor hub held by three arms.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a sliding-mesh URANS and FW-H set-up for the fan (OpenFOAM, libAcoustics), with the failed
    non-conformal-coupling attempt and the fixes that made the cyclicAMI run stable;
  \item a per-record spectral analysis of the FW-H signals, which corrects two errors of the first analysis and
    identifies a once-per-revolution tone whose directivity matches a rotating in-plane force;
  \item a consistency check of the FW-H levels against the integrated blade forces, which shows that the
    absolute tone levels of this run are not reliable.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

The Ffowcs Williams--Hawkings equation \citep{ffowcswilliams1969} extends Lighthill's analogy to sources on
moving surfaces. For surfaces moving at subsonic speed, Farassat's Formulation 1A gives the thickness and loading
noise as retarded-time surface integrals with the observer-time derivative taken analytically
\citep{farassat2007}; it is the standard tool for rotor tonal noise \citep{brentner2003}. A 2026 preprint argues
that Formulation 1A omits a term linked to the variation of the surface normal, which matters for surfaces that
do not move in pure translation \citep{fwh1a_preprint2026}; this was not examined here. The FW-H implementation
used is libAcoustics \citep{epikhin2015,libacoustics}, a library of function objects for OpenFOAM
\citep{weller1998,openfoam}. Rotor motion uses the arbitrary mesh interface (AMI), whose conservative
interpolation between non-matching meshes follows the local Galerkin projection of \citet{farrell2011}. The flow
uses the $k$--$\omega$ SST model \citep{menter1994}. On serrations, \citet{howe1991} gave the first analytical
model of sawtooth trailing-edge noise and \citet{lyu2016} a more complete prediction; both concern broadband
noise, which a URANS simulation does not resolve. Spectral estimation follows the windowing practice of
\citet{harris1978}; averaging periodograms over segments is Welch's method \citep{welch1967}.

\subsection{Equations}

The incompressible Navier--Stokes equations are solved with the $k$--$\omega$ SST closure \citep{menter1994}. The
rotor zone turns as a rigid body; fluxes cross the cylindrical interface ($R=\SI{72}{mm}$) through the AMI. The
acoustic pressure at observer $\mathbf{x}$ is obtained from the FW-H equation with the blade skin as the
integration surface, keeping the thickness and loading terms and neglecting the quadrupole term:
\begin{align}
  4\pi p'_T(\mathbf{x},t) &= \int_S\left[\frac{\rho_0(\dot v_n+v_{\dot n})}{r(1-M_r)^2}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}}\!\mathrm{d}S
  +\int_S\left[\frac{\rho_0 v_n\,(r\dot M_r+c_0M_r-c_0M^2)}{r^2(1-M_r)^3}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}}\!\mathrm{d}S,\\
  4\pi p'_L(\mathbf{x},t) &= \frac{1}{c_0}\int_S\left[\frac{\dot\ell_r}{r(1-M_r)^2}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}}\!\mathrm{d}S
  +\int_S\left[\frac{\ell_r-\ell_M}{r^2(1-M_r)^2}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}}\!\mathrm{d}S \nonumber\\
  &\quad+\frac{1}{c_0}\int_S\left[\frac{\ell_r\,(r\dot M_r+c_0M_r-c_0M^2)}{r^2(1-M_r)^3}\right]_{\mathrm{ret}}\!\mathrm{d}S,
\end{align}
with $p'=p'_T+p'_L$, in the notation of \citet{farassat2007} and \citet{brentner2003}: $v_n$ is the normal
surface velocity, $\ell_r$ and $\ell_M$ are the surface loading projected on the radiation direction and on the
surface Mach number vector, $M_r$ is the Mach number in the radiation direction, and dots denote derivatives with
respect to source time. Because the solver is incompressible, the surface pressure is a kinematic pressure; the
FW-H input uses $\rho_0=\SI{1.2}{kg/m^3}$ and $c_0=\SI{343}{m/s}$.

\section{Numerical set-up}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Mesh}

The mesh is built with \texttt{snappyHexMesh} from a \SI{10}{mm} background grid, refined to level 5
(\SI{0.31}{mm}) on the blades so that the serrations are resolved, level 3 on the duct and arms and level 2 on
the hub, with a refined rotor cylinder and wake region (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh}). The faceZone of the rotor
cylinder is split into a cyclicAMI pair with \texttt{createBaffles}. The retained mesh has
\num{1949584} cells; its quality is listed in Table~\ref{tab:checkmesh}. The intended low-Reynolds prism layers
could not be inserted on the thin serrated blades, so the blade $y^+$ averages about 4 and reaches 445.

\subsection{Solver and time strategy}

\texttt{pimpleFoam} is run with three outer correctors, two pressure correctors and one non-orthogonal
corrector. Convection of momentum uses \texttt{linearUpwindV}; $k$ and $\omega$ use \texttt{limitedLinear};
time integration is first-order implicit Euler. The run has two phases. Phase 1, from rest to \SI{0.19}{s}
(about 4.8 revolutions), uses an adaptive time step limited by a Courant number of 3 and
$\Delta t\le\SI{1e-4}{s}$, with the FW-H function object off. Phase 2 restarts with a fixed
$\Delta t=\SI{3.5e-5}{s}$ and the FW-H function object on, and runs to \SI{0.39}{s}. The fixed step is confirmed
by the force output, which is written every 10 steps at a uniform interval of \SI{3.5e-4}{s}. The FW-H pressure
is sampled every 5 steps ($f_s=\SI{5714}{Hz}$) and written in two records of \SI{100}{ms}.

The degenerate cells make the solver stop with a floating-point exception at the first step unless
\texttt{FOAM\_SIGFPE=false} is set; this tolerates them without changing their quality.

\subsection{How the set-up was reached}

Steady multiple-reference-frame runs at \num{3000} and then \SI{4320}{rpm} (end of May 2026) gave first flow
rates. The first transient attempt used the non-conformal coupling of OpenFOAM.org: over six mesh variants
(re-ordered interfaces, Delaunay and rounded end caps of the rotor cylinder) the runs stopped in the mesh
stitcher after about \SI{200}{\micro s} of simulated time. The case was then moved to the ESI OpenFOAM release with
cyclicAMI (12 June 2026). Two further changes were needed: meshing in serial, because the parallel
\texttt{snappyHexMesh} aborted while load-balancing the AMI faceZone, and a PCG solver instead of GAMG for the
pressure on the coarse test mesh, where a zero-determinant cell made GAMG ill-conditioned.

\subsection{Acoustic post-processing}\label{sec:post}

Each FW-H record begins with a start-up transient: on the axial microphones its peak reaches 35 to 52 robust
standard deviations of the rest of the record. The first \SI{15}{ms} of each record are discarded, which leaves
two segments of \SI{85}{ms} (486 samples) separated by a \SI{15}{ms} gap (sim.\ time \SIrange{0.205}{0.290}{s}
and \SIrange{0.305}{0.390}{s}). Each segment has its mean and linear trend removed, is Hann-windowed and
zero-padded to 8192 points; the two power spectra are averaged. The true frequency resolution is
\SI{11.8}{Hz}. Tone levels are the spectrum peaks within $\pm\SI{6}{Hz}$ of $n$ and of the BPF; the local
floor is the median level between 140 and \SI{240}{Hz}.

\subsection{Computing}

The runs used a two-node Linux cluster built for this project (8 and 4 cores, MPI over Ethernet, shared NFS
directory). A \texttt{simpleFoam} benchmark on \num{782400} cells showed that one node saturates at 4 cores
(1.51 times faster than 1 core; 1.44 times on 8 cores) and that 12 cores on both nodes reach 1.93 times. For the
AMI case, splitting one run across the two nodes gave no gain, so the cluster was used to run independent cases
side by side. A logged stretch of phase~1 (961 steps) took \SI{15844}{s} of CPU time, about \SI{16.5}{s} per
step.

\section{Verification}

\paragraph{Established regime.} The thrust varies by less than 1\,\% from one revolution to the next after about
4.75 revolutions, before the FW-H recording starts (Figure~\ref{fig:loads}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering

\caption{Thrust and torque on the blades: start-up transient, then a periodic regime.}

\paragraph{Post-processing audit.} The first analysis of these records concatenated the two segments and
applied a 5-point median filter against numerical spikes. Both choices were re-examined. The concatenation joins
two segments \SI{15}{ms} apart, which puts a phase jump of $360^\circ\times\SI{15}{ms}\times\SI{25.15}{Hz}
\approx 136^\circ$ into the once-per-revolution component and splits its peak. The segments contain few outlier
samples (at most 4 beyond 5 robust standard deviations), and the filter raises the floor by 9 to \SI{24}{dB},
hides the BPF harmonics and moves the radial BPF level by about \SI{4}{dB} (Figure~\ref{fig:filter}). The results
below use the per-segment, unfiltered processing of Section~
ef{sec:post}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{raw_vs_filtered.pdf}
\caption{Radial microphone, average of the two segments: raw signal (used) and 5-point median filter (not used).}
\label{fig:filter}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Coherence between records.} On the radial and $45^\circ$ microphones, a sine fit at \SI{25.15}{Hz} on
the absolute time axis gives the same level (21.0 and \SI{17.9}{dB}) and the same phase ($148^\circ$) in both
segments: the once-per-revolution tone is a steady feature of the run. On the axial microphones the fitted phase
changes from one segment to the other and the level stays at the floor.

\paragraph{Mesh quality.} See Table~\ref{tab:checkmesh}. The effect of the tolerated cells on the spectra was not
measured.

\paragraph{Not verified.} No mesh-independence study (one mesh retained), no second time step, no resolved
boundary layer on the blades, and no comparison with a measurement.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Aerodynamics}

Averaged over the last \SI{50}{ms} of the force record, the rotor gives \SI{43.3}{mN} of thrust and
\SI{1.023}{mN.m} of torque, that is an aerodynamic power $C\Omega=\SI{0.162}{W}$. The flow rate through a cut
\SI{60}{mm} downstream of the rotor is \SI{30.1}{L/s} (\SI{108}{m^3/h}). The blades drive an annular jet along
the duct wall, with a slow recirculating wake behind the hub (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}).

\subsection{Origin of the once-per-revolution tone, and a check of the levels}\label{sec:levels}

The net force on the blades has an in-plane component of \SI{0.95}{mN} that rotates with the rotor: $F_x$ and
$F_y$ oscillate at \SI{25.15}{Hz} with amplitudes \num{0.945} and \SI{0.948}{mN}. At \SI{25}{Hz} the rotor is
compact (wavelength \SI{13.6}{m}), and a compact dipole rotating in the rotor plane radiates nothing on the axis
and $\cos 45^\circ$ of its in-plane amplitude at $45^\circ$, that is $-\SI{3.0}{dB}$. The FW-H data give no tone on
the axis and a $45^\circ$ level \SI{3.1}{dB} below the radial one. The directivity of the tone is therefore that of
the rotating in-plane force. Whether that force is physical (a blade-to-blade difference of the printed
geometry) or a mesh effect was not established.

The levels do not agree. Table~\ref{tab:dipole} compares the FW-H levels with the compact-dipole estimate
$|p'|=A\,(4\pi r)^{-1}\sqrt{(\omega/c_0)^2+r^{-2}}$ computed from the force amplitudes $A$ over the same time
window (script \path{paper/scripts/dipole_check.py}). FW-H is \SI{12}{dB} above the estimate at
\SI{25}{Hz} and \SI{18}{dB} below it at the BPF on the axis. The two discrepancies have opposite signs, so a single
unit factor (such as the kinematic-pressure convention) cannot explain both. Candidate causes are the treatment
of the rotating integration surface in the FW-H function object and the omitted surface-normal term discussed by
\citep{fwh1a_preprint2026}; neither was tested. Until this is resolved, the tone frequencies, their presence on
each microphone and the $25$-Hz directivity are the results of this study, not the absolute levels.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

The chain produces a physically consistent flow field and a spectrum with the expected structure: BPF and
harmonics everywhere, plus a once-per-revolution tone whose directivity is explained by the measured force
history. The quantitative acoustic results are weaker than the aerodynamic ones, for five reasons.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Inconsistent levels.} The FW-H levels disagree with the force-based estimate in both directions
    (Table~\ref{tab:dipole}).
  \item \emph{Resolution.} Two \SI{85}{ms} segments give an \SI{11.8}{Hz} resolution; the once-per-revolution tone
    at \SI{25}{Hz} sits only two bins from zero. A contiguous \SI{0.4}{s} record would need about
    \num{11400} steps, roughly 55 to \SI{60}{h} at the measured cost, and the cluster that holds the restart data
    was offline when this report was written.
  \item \emph{Numerical accuracy.} One mesh, one time step, first-order time integration at Courant numbers up to
    3 in phase~1, no prism layers on the blades, and \num{49827} concave cells.
  \item \emph{Physics.} URANS resolves tonal noise only. The serrations act mainly on broadband noise
    \citep{howe1991,lyu2016}, and no smooth-blade reference was run, so this study does not measure their effect.
    The FW-H surface covers the blades only; the duct, arms and hub are not part of it.
  \item \emph{No measurement.} Thrust, flow rate and spectra are not compared with tests.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and future work}

A sliding-mesh URANS and FW-H chain was set up for a serrated ducted fan and run on a two-node cluster; it gives
\SI{43.3}{mN} of thrust and \SI{108}{m^3/h} at \SI{1509}{rpm}, a BPF tone with harmonics on all microphones and a
once-per-revolution tone confined to the rotor plane. Re-processing the FW-H records per segment corrected two
analysis errors, and a force-based compact-dipole check showed that the absolute FW-H levels of this run are not
reliable. In order of priority, the next steps are: find the cause of the level discrepancy on a case with a known
answer (a rotating compact dipole, or a single rotating blade); record a contiguous signal of at least
\SI{0.4}{s}; run two more meshes and a second time step; add prism layers; and run a smooth-blade reference rotor
under the same conditions before drawing any conclusion on the serrations.
